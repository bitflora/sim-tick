The research base is reasonably strong that entomopathogenic fungi can kill ticks under laboratory and semi-field conditions. The main question is not whether they work at all — they clearly do — but whether they can produce durable, cost-effective suppression in real outdoor environments.

The most studied fungi are:

* Metarhizium anisopliae
* Beauveria bassiana

## Overall effectiveness

Typical reported outcomes:

| Context                              | Typical mortality / reduction  |
| ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------ |
| Controlled laboratory assays         | 60–100% tick mortality         |
| Semi-field grass/soil experiments    | 60–95% mortality               |
| Outdoor field population suppression | ~50–70% reduction              |
| Dry/sunny environments               | Often much lower effectiveness |

The strongest and most repeatable effects occur:

* at high humidity,
* moderate temperatures,
* shaded environments,
* and when fungi directly contact questing ticks.

## Important studies and numbers

### 1. Kaaya et al. (2001) — livestock tick control

Researchers tested fungal oil formulations against African livestock ticks.

Results:

* **100% larval mortality**
* **80–100% nymph mortality**
* **80–90% adult mortality**

using oil formulations containing fungal spores at (10^9) conidia/mL. ([PubMed][1])

The same studies found:

* egg hatchability reductions up to **100%**
* fecundity reductions of **85–99%** in some tick species. ([PubMed][2])

These are among the most impressive results in the literature, though they were achieved under highly favorable moisture conditions.

## 2. Longhorned tick soil treatment study (2019)

A Korean study on Haemaphysalis longicornis tested soil-surface spraying with Metarhizium anisopliae.

Results:

* **60–90% mortality after 30 days**
* strongest isolates were clearly dose-dependent
* some Beauveria bassiana strains showed little pathogenicity in this species. ([ScienceDirect][3])

This study is important because:

* it used a realistic environmental substrate (soil),
* and targeted off-host ticks where fungi are more likely to persist.

## 3. Blacklegged tick (Lyme vector) studies

Research on Ixodes scapularis found:

* **100% mortality** of engorged larvae and females at high spore concentrations
* LC50 around (10^7) spores/mL for larvae. ([USGS][4])

Another study found:

* **50–70% mortality** in adult ticks,
* and >90% mortality in some nymphal stages after 21–28 days. ([OUP Academic][5])

These results matter because:

* *Ixodes scapularis* is the primary vector for Lyme disease in eastern North America.

## 4. Species-dependent susceptibility

One of the major findings across the literature is that different tick species respond very differently.

Example:

* Amblyomma maculatum showed:

  * > 90% mortality with *Beauveria*
* while Amblyomma americanum showed:

  * <10–15% mortality under the same conditions. ([ScienceDirect][6])

This variability is a major obstacle to broad deployment.

## 5. European tick studies

A 2020 study on:

* Ixodes ricinus
* Dermacentor reticulatus

reported:

* up to **100% mortality** with some strains,
* but large differences between males and females,
* and substantial strain-to-strain variability. ([PMC][7])

The best-performing isolate had LC50 values as low as:
[
2.6 \times 10^3 \text{ cfu/mL}
]
for female *Ixodes ricinus*. ([PMC][7])

## 6. Modern field-trial estimates

A recent 2025 review/study reported:

* ~**67.3% reduction** in field tick populations after fungal treatment,
* with *Metarhizium* somewhat outperforming *Beauveria*. ([PubMed][8])

It also reported:

* LT50 (time to kill 50% of ticks):

  * 5.8 days for *Metarhizium*
  * 6.9 days for *Beauveria*. ([PubMed][8])

## Why fungi often fail outside the lab

The recurring problem is environmental fragility.

Major limiting factors:

* UV light destroys spores
* low humidity prevents germination
* rain can wash spores away
* ticks spend much of their time hidden under leaf litter
* fungal persistence outdoors is inconsistent

This is why:

* lab mortality can approach 100%,
* while real-world suppression is usually more modest.

## Current scientific consensus

The consensus appears to be:

1. Fungal biocontrol absolutely can kill ticks effectively.
2. *Metarhizium anisopliae* generally performs better than *Beauveria bassiana* against many tick species.
3. Environmental persistence is the main engineering challenge.
4. Fungi are most promising as part of integrated tick management rather than standalone eradication tools. ([PMC][9])

## Best practical deployment ideas under investigation

Researchers are currently exploring:

* fungal-treated bait boxes for rodents,
* slow-release formulations,
* UV-protective carriers,
* soil granules,
* leaf-litter applications,
* and combining fungi with low-dose acaricides.

The most plausible near-term use case is probably:

* reducing local tick density in shaded residential woodland edges,
* rather than broad landscape-wide eradication.
